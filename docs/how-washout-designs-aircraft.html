How Washout Designs Aircraft

A program that takes 35 numbers, turns them into a flying wing, proves the aeroplane flies and prints, and repeats that half a million times — then hands you STL files. Here is every part of it, what each part is worth, and what went wrong along the way.

The idea in one paragraph

You asked for aircraft that design themselves, get tested in your own wind tunnel, and come out as printable files. The program does that by writing down an aeroplane as a list of numbers, building the real geometry from those numbers, and then running every check a person would run — does it balance, does it fly at a sensible speed, will the wing hold, will it fit on the bed, does it wobble — and scoring the result. A search algorithm then spends hundreds of thousands of guesses hunting for the numbers that pass everything and fly best.

The interesting part is not the search. It is that every check has to be true of the aircraft you would actually build. Nearly every problem in this project came from some check quietly judging a different aeroplane than the one being exported.

The pipeline

One design, from 35 numbers to a verdict. The cheap checks come first on purpose, and the expensive ones only run on shapes that have earned them.

  1. Geometry from numbers

    Five stations along the half-wing set chord, sweep, dihedral, twist and section shape. The loft runs through the slopes and integrates them, so sweep and dihedral can never overshoot what was asked for, and the two halves meet smoothly at the centreline.

  2. Is it one shape?

    A fairness check measures the built surface for waves, hooks, reversals and lumps. It costs 50 ms and rejects about 45% of random designs before any physics runs.

  3. Slice it and weigh it

    The wing is cut into printable panels and the shell weight comes from the actual length of plastic bead in the sliced layers, not an estimate. Battery, motor, receiver, servos, spars and fins are added at their real positions to get the balance point.

  4. Make it fly

    A vortex lattice finds the angle where the wing balances itself, which fixes the lift coefficient, which fixes the flying speed. Drag comes from wind-tunnel measurements, charged strip by strip at each strip's own Reynolds number.

  5. Check everything else

    Thrust available against drag needed, elevon authority, battery bay depth, spar reach, stall behaviour, wing loading, balance margin, heading stability and the Dutch roll damping.

  6. Add the structure, then judge again

    The spar is picked from stock carbon tube and the rib spacing from what the skin needs to not buckle. Those add 8–22 g, mostly behind the balance point, so the whole evaluation runs a second time with them fitted. That verdict is the one that counts.

What the models cannot see

The lattice is inviscid: it has no stall and no separation, so maximum lift comes from the measured section data instead. Spanwise boundary-layer drift is invisible to both models, which is why I advised against wing fences — their benefit cannot be scored here, so the optimizer would pay real weight and drag for an invented gain. The 3D tunnel run that could see it never completed; it hung the virtual machine your trading bot runs in, and I stopped rather than retry.

What "the wobble dies out" means

A gust makes a tailless wing yaw and roll together. The damping ratio says whether that oscillation shrinks or grows, and it is the check that reshaped the whole fleet.

Nothing checked this until two days ago. The old measure compared two stiffnesses and called it stability, which passed a trainer whose wobble grows. The replacement solves the actual motion — sideslip, roll rate, yaw rate and bank angle together — and reports how fast the oscillation decays. That single check is why the fleet now carries tip fins.

Scoring is strictly ordered rather than weighted. Any fair shape outranks any unfair one; among fair shapes, any flyable design outranks any unflyable one; only then does performance decide. A weighted sum lets the optimizer buy its way past a rule it should not be allowed to break — which it did, early on, by flying slightly too slowly and paying for it in efficiency.

How it gets printed

Each wing half is cut into four panels, printed on end so the span runs up the Z axis, in spiral vase mode: one continuous bead, no infill, no top or bottom layers. That gives a light, seamless skin, but it allows exactly one closed loop per layer — which is what makes the internals interesting.

  • Ribs are not separate parts. The single loop detours inward, runs across to within one bead of the far skin, and comes back, so the two legs weld into a web. As the detour moves layer by layer, the webs sweep into a diagonal truss. Spacing comes from what the skin needs to resist buckling: 16–46 mm depending on the aircraft.
  • Spars need no hole. The shell's own cavity is the bore, because the span is the print axis. The search keeps a clear corridor for an 8 mm tube and checks every panel joint is deep enough to pass one; ribs are nudged aside so they never cross it.
  • Tip fins are the exception: a plate standing across the span cannot come out of that spiral, so they print flat on the bed in normal mode and glue to the tips.

Every exported mesh is checked watertight before it is written, and each panel is fitted to the bed at the angle that makes it fit.

They were printed as-is by choice, rather than re-searched.

The fuselage

There isn't one. The centre body is part of the wing, and the panel split sits exactly at the body/wing junction — the p0 pair, mirrored and glued at the centreline, is the fuselage. Everything lives inside it: battery, receiver, ESC and servos, each with a volume the search checked would fit.

Print p0 with zero bottom layers. The exported mesh is a closed solid, capped at both ends, so default vase settings would seal the root — and the spar has to run tip to tip straight through that face, with the electronics loaded through it before the halves are joined.

Known weak spots

  • The micro is the one to watch on a first flight: ζ +0.070 is below the Level 1 threshold, so its yaw-roll wobble damps out slowly. The AR630's AS3X gyro will cover it in practice; the model does not count gyros.
  • Every aircraft here needs nearly the largest tip fins allowed to reach the damping it has. Fins are the only lateral lever the search has, and it spends all of it.
  • demon1 is the hardest mission to satisfy — one of its three attempts found nothing flyable, tripping over battery bay depth and propeller tip speed.
  • The micro is still pinned against the smallest span I allowed it, so the true minimum size is smaller and unknown.
  • Elevon hinges, servo mounting and the motor firewall are not in the geometry. They are real mechanical decisions and I left them for you rather than invent them.

Running it

# search one mission, seeded from a previous winner
python run.py search --mission trainer_v3 --iters 45 --popsize 6 --seed 0 \
    --polar "out/tunnel/polar.csv@60000,out/tunnel_re100/polar.csv@100000" \
    --seed-design out/gen5/trainer_v3_v101/design.json --out out/gen6/trainer

# re-export a saved design (spar and ribs sized the same way)
python run.py export --design out/gen5/demon1_v102/design.json --mission demon1 \
    --polar "out/tunnel/polar.csv@60000,out/tunnel_re100/polar.csv@100000" \
    --out out/gen5/demon1_v102

# the whole fleet, one search per core
bash scripts/run_fleet_gen5.sh

# every check that pins a past mistake
python -m pytest tests/ -q

A full search is 9,660 designs and takes six to ten hours on your machine, run several at a time, one core each.
